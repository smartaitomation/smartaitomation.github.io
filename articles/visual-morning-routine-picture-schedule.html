How to make a visual morning routine with picture steps

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 5, 20264 min read

Choose a short part of the morning, put its steps in the order they actually happen, and pair each short label with a picture the person understands. Show the first step, then mark it finished and move to the next. Start with fewer steps if the full sequence is overwhelming. The original printable cards below are a starting point; replace them with meaningful pictures or your own photos.

Choose a useful sequence, not the whole day

Start where uncertainty occurs. If getting dressed is the difficult transition, a three-step board may be more useful than a schedule from waking up to bedtime. The order below is one example and should change to match your home, needs and preferences. Avoid including a task just because it appears on a generic printable.

Wake up
Get dressed
Eat breakfast
Brush teeth
Pack bag
Leave home

These simple symbols are illustrative. They may not communicate the task to every person. A photo of the person’s actual backpack or toothbrush may be clearer. Use the print button to print this example, or edit the labels in the HTML cards supplied with this guide. Use your own photos in PictoCue or a separate layout tool.

Teach the board before expecting it to work

Introduce the pictures during a calm moment. Name a step, show the corresponding action and explain how finished steps are marked. Refer back to the board while doing the routine, rather than presenting it once and assuming the symbols are understood.

The early-childhood visual-support resource linked below recommends explaining and demonstrating the schedule and moving or turning cards when tasks are finished. The sample here applies that general approach; it is not a treatment plan or proof that this particular board changes behavior.

  • Place the board where it can be seen at the point of use.
  • Use short familiar labels: “shoes on” may be clearer than “prepare for departure.”
  • Mark completion consistently, such as moving a card to a finished area.
  • Change the board visibly when the plan changes, rather than expecting the old picture to mean something new.

When a first/then board is clearer

A first/then board displays two immediate steps rather than the whole morning. For example: first put shoes on, then leave for the park. Both steps should be truthful and achievable. Do not promise a preferred activity that is unavailable simply to encourage compliance.

A choice board answers a different question: which option can I choose? Keep it separate from the order-of-events board when that distinction matters. Give the person a way to show that a step is unclear or needs help. A schedule should explain what comes next, not make every unexpected delay look like failure.

Questions answered

How many steps should a morning board have?

Use the shortest sequence that answers the person’s next question. Start small and add steps only when useful.

Can I use my own pictures?

Yes. PictoCue’s current listing supports photo cards; familiar real objects may be clearer than generic symbols.

Does a visual schedule treat autism or ADHD?

This guide and PictoCue describe an organizational aid, not diagnosis or treatment. Individual support needs vary.
