Film a tennis forehand: a one-cue video review and practice plan

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 8, 20265 min read

Film from a safe, stable position where your full body, racket and contact area stay in frame. Review one representative forehand at normal speed and then slowly. Note a single observable cue, such as when the shoulders turn or where contact happens relative to your body. Test one drill on court, then film again under similar conditions. A score from one clip is not a measurement of your overall tennis ability.

Capture a clip you can actually compare

Ask a partner to film only when it is safe, or place the phone outside the playing area on a stable support. Do not hold it while rallying. Get permission before recording another player, especially a child. Keep the entire hitting action visible: preparation, footwork, contact and follow-through. A dramatic close-up can hide the part you need to inspect.

Choose a normal rally ball before selecting an unusually good or bad shot. Record the camera position, approximate distance and drill so the next clip is comparable. Poor lighting, an obscured contact point or a clip ending before follow-through make a confident conclusion less likely. Retake the clip rather than filling gaps with guesses.

Review one cue, not a whole technique score

First play the clip at normal speed and write what you actually see. Then pause near preparation and contact. The USTA teaches that a forehand starts with a coordinated body turn and that beginners should attend to contact position and racket-face control. These are useful things to observe; one angle may still conceal exact biomechanics.

Turn one observation into a small drill

If your cue is preparation, the USTA unit-turn progression begins by turning hips and upper body together, then rehearsing the movement before hitting. Do a short, controlled set with a partner or coach. Afterward, film from approximately the same position and compare the same moment. Note whether the cue changed, whether the ball remained in play and what to practice next. Stop adding cues when the drill becomes confusing.

If the issue is spacing, use the contact-position guidance in USTA’s forehand basics as a question to bring to a coach. A coach can account for grip, incoming ball, mobility and tactical goal in a way a single video cannot. The useful unit of progress here is a repeatable observation and next action, not a guaranteed numerical improvement.

Questions answered

What camera angle is best for a forehand?

Use an angle that keeps the whole movement and contact visible while the phone stays out of the playing area. If the point you need to judge is hidden, film a second safe angle.

How much video do I need to review?

One representative stroke is enough to choose one cue. Keep enough time before and after contact to see preparation and follow-through.

Can AI tell me exactly how good my forehand is?

No. It can suggest what to inspect, but angle, lighting, clip selection and model error limit any single-clip estimate.
