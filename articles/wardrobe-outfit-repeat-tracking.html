Track outfit repeats to learn which pieces you use

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 26, 20267 min read

Log what you actually wore with stable item IDs and a short context note, then count repeats only within the days you recorded. Keep planned outfits, unknown days and clothing waiting for care separate. A short wear log can reveal practical patterns without proving that an unworn piece is useless or that a purchase is necessary.

Define a wear record before counting anything

Choose a small observation window and a rule you can apply consistently. For this worksheet, an item worn on one recorded day counts once, even if it appears in two outfits that day. This is our bookkeeping convention, not a universal clothing standard. Keep the original date and item identity so the same photograph does not create a second wear.

Record the pieces actually worn, not merely saved or suggested. A planned combination may change because of weather, comfort, laundry or a different activity. Keep that change visible instead of copying the plan into the wear history. An unrecorded day is unknown, not proof that nothing was worn.

Add a brief context and readiness note: work, walk, dinner, repair needed or waiting for care. Follow the garment’s own care instructions rather than using a wear count to prescribe washing frequency. The FTC’s US care-label guidance concerns instructions for covered apparel; it does not establish a number of wears before cleaning.

Worked week: five recorded days and two unknown days

Fictional closet IDs are T01 blue shirt, B01 dark trousers, J01 jacket and S01 shoes. The owner recorded Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, Friday and Sunday. Wednesday and Saturday were not logged. The worksheet preserves those gaps.

Within the five recorded days, T01 appears on two, B01 on four, J01 on two and S01 on five. These are item-day counts, not four complete-outfit repeat counts and not a percentage of the entire closet. Do not divide by seven and pretend the two unknown days were checked.

The complete listed combination T01+B01+S01 repeats on Monday and Thursday. Tuesday and Sunday both include J01 and S01, but their full outfits are not established as identical. Sunday explicitly leaves another top outside this sample. A shared pair of items does not prove the whole outfit repeated.

The shirt’s absence on Friday has a recorded care reason. That is different from disliking it. A formal outfit not observed in one ordinary week may still serve an important occasion. Keep the observation bounded to its dates and activities before making a purchase or disposal decision.

Use the pattern to ask one useful next question

Choose a practical follow-up: does a frequently used piece need a repair, is a favorite combination worth planning again, or was a low count explained by the season? Keep the decision separate from the count so a later reader can understand the reason.

If you add earlier wears from memory, mark them as estimates rather than mixing them into the checked week. If two closet records describe the same garment, resolve the identity before adding their counts. Keep similarly colored but physically different items separate.

No carbon saving, cost saving, lifetime durability, body-fit accuracy or style score is calculated here. Those would require other evidence. The weekly wardrobe guide helps choose future outfits; this guide compares future intent with what was actually observed after the week.

Questions answered

Does a saved outfit mean I wore it?

No. Record actual wear separately from ideas and plans.

Are two unknown days the same as zero wears?

No. Keep the observation gap visible.

Should I discard a piece after a low-count week?

A short context-limited sample is not enough to decide that by itself.
