What to change when a drum rudiment stops improving

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 10, 20266 min read

Before increasing BPM again, define what a clean repeat means and check the sticking, subdivision and sound at a comfortable tempo. Keep those conditions fixed while testing one change, such as a smaller tempo step. Record a repeat or too-fast result honestly; the highest metronome number is not automatically your reliable playing speed.

Make the plateau observable

“I am stuck at 100” leaves important information missing. Does the click represent quarter notes? Are you playing sixteenth notes? Which rudiment and lead hand are you using? Are you losing spacing, accents, sticking or comfort? Write these details before changing the routine.

Vic Firth’s drum education sampler recommends starting slowly with a metronome and aiming for an even, relaxed stroke. It does not prescribe the experiment or BPM values below. This guide offers a personal comparison method, not a promise that a particular exercise will break a plateau.

Choose one audible cue: for example, even spacing with the stated sticking across a short passage. Define a repeat as unsuccessful if the pattern changes, you rush away from the click or the cue becomes inconsistent. Do not turn the test into playing through pain or unusual strain; stop and get appropriate guidance for a recurring technique or comfort problem.

Change one setting while holding the others steady

Keep the same rudiment, pad or instrument, subdivision, passage length and selected cue. If your ladder jumps too far to keep that cue consistent, test a smaller step. Do not also switch sticking, add accents and extend the passage in the same comparison. Otherwise, you cannot tell which change mattered.

A pass describes the selected passage today; it does not certify every duration, accent pattern or lead-hand version at that BPM. Interpret the result without chasing a score

If the smaller step still fails, return to a tempo where the cue remains reliable and identify the exact moment the pattern slips. A specific note—“left-hand diddle compresses in bar four”—is more useful for a lesson than “speed bad.” Ask a qualified teacher to check technique if the same problem recurs.

If the passage passes, repeat the same conditions in a later session before describing it as a dependable personal benchmark. Do not compare your quarter-note-click sixteenth notes with another drummer’s eighth-note exercise at the same BPM: the numbers refer to different tasks.

Keep session length realistic. Fatigue, changing surfaces and a different subdivision can make two observations hard to compare. Label those changes rather than treating every lower result as lost progress. The next useful question is whether the chosen cue remains stable, not how to make the history chart rise every day.

Questions answered

Does the same BPM mean the same hand speed?

No. The click unit and note subdivision matter. Write both before comparing sessions or another exercise.

Should I count an uneven attempt as a personal best?

Keep it as an attempt, with its actual observation. Reserve a clean benchmark for the conditions you defined.

Will smaller tempo steps guarantee improvement?

No. They make a personal experiment easier to interpret; persistent technique problems may need instruction.
