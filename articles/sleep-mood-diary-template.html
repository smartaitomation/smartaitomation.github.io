Sleep and morning mood diary: a one-week template you can actually finish

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 7, 20263 min read

For one week, record when you tried to sleep, when you woke, your estimated sleep quality, morning mood and one or two habits that may matter to you. Keep the scale consistent and review the pattern after several days. A diary is a conversation aid, not a diagnosis, and a graph of two measures does not prove that one caused the other.

Keep the daily entry small

Use the same simple fields each day: date, estimated bedtime, wake time, perceived quality on a scale you define, morning mood on a consistent scale, and one note. Add caffeine, alcohol, medication or an unusual event only when it helps the question you are exploring. If you missed a night, leave a gap rather than inventing a value.

The NIH/NHLBI sleep diary records sleep quality, quantity and factors such as medicines, alcohol and caffeine for later discussion with a doctor. The compact version here focuses on a routine you can sustain; use a fuller clinical diary if your clinician asks for one.

Review with a useful question

After seven days, ask a question that can change your routine: “Are my shortest nights clustered before early shifts?” or “Do I rate mornings differently after late caffeine?” Avoid turning the chart into a diagnosis of insomnia or anxiety. If you are frequently sleepy, have breathing concerns, or your sleep affects daily safety, bring the diary to a qualified professional.

If you use a wearable, compare its imported duration with your own notes without assuming either is perfect. Devices estimate sleep using their own sensors and algorithms; your morning entry captures a different kind of information.

Questions answered

Do I need a wearable?

No. A consistent manual estimate and morning note can still be useful.

What if I forget a day?

Leave it blank and resume. Invented data makes the pattern less useful.

Can a sleep diary diagnose insomnia?

No. Share persistent concerns with a qualified professional, who may use a more complete assessment.
