Separate a guitar timing problem from a difficult chord change

Hold the pulse and strumming pattern constant while simplifying the fretting, then compare what you hear. A cleaner simplified attempt suggests a useful next check; it does not prove the cause of an error or produce an automatic playing score.

Describe one observable problem before changing the exercise

“My rhythm is bad” is too broad to guide a useful repetition. Write what you actually noticed: an attack seemed late at the chord change, the pulse was hard to hear, or a note buzzed while the beat continued. Timing, note clarity and coordination can overlap. Keep the observation separate from a conclusion such as “my left hand caused it.”

Choose a very short original exercise: two bars of 4/4, one comfortable downstroke on each quarter-note click, with a change between two familiar chords at the start of bar two. Pick a comfortable tempo and write it down. No specific BPM is required to diagnose the task. Stop if you have pain or discomfort rather than treating discomfort as a practice target.

Fender’s own practice guidance describes getting comfortable with finger placement and then using a slow metronome setting. That supports separating manageable technique work from tempo demands. It does not validate the example below as a scientific diagnostic test, and Fender’s product features are not RiffSprint features.

Three passes that change one demand at a time

Keep the same instrument setup, two-bar length, pulse and one-downstroke-per-click rhythm. Make a short pass, pause and note what you heard. The following observations are fictional examples, not measured performance data.

A/B/C is a comparison of task conditions, not a proof that one hand is faulty. If the late entry disappears in B and returns in C, the change deserves focused attention. If timing also drifts in B, simplify further or ask a teacher to listen before assuming the chord transition explains everything.

Do not count a rest or an intentional gap as an error unless it differs from your written pattern. Likewise, a buzzing note can occur on time. Give timing and note clarity separate fields so an overall “bad attempt” label does not erase the useful distinction.

Choose a smaller next action and keep unknowns visible

For the fictional result above, rehearse the two chord shapes slowly without the rhythm demand, then return to the exact short pattern. If you change tempo for the next pass, record that new condition rather than comparing it as though nothing changed. For a dedicated transition log, use the chord-change guide.

If you cannot hear whether attacks align, write “not assessed.” A timer finishing, a metronome running or a session being saved does not fill that gap. A teacher or a recording method you choose can provide another observation, but do not invent a millisecond offset from memory. This worksheet deliberately has no automatic timing-accuracy formula.

Keep a small repeatable question for the next session: “Does the same entry feel late when I restore the change?” That is more testable than “be better at rhythm.” The short practice plan can help fit that question into a session without turning every pass into a speed challenge.

Questions answered

Does a better simplified pass prove my fretting hand is the problem?

No. It identifies a condition worth checking, while other factors remain possible. Keep the heard result and your hypothesis separate.

Can RiffSprint listen and score the exercise?

Its current listing explicitly says no microphone recording. This guide does not claim automatic playing analysis.

Should I increase BPM after one good pass?

This comparison is about identifying the task, not prescribing a speed progression. Record a reliable observation and choose the next small check.
