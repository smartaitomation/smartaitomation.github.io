Separate the receipt from your allocation choices

A split can look reasonable while failing its final check. Three rounded shares of $12.83 add to $38.49, even when the bill paid was $38.50. The missing cent is arithmetic; changing the tip to hide it makes the record harder to explain. Work in one currency with two decimal places for this example. Cash-rounding laws and currency-specific rules are outside this worksheet.

First copy the item subtotal, actual receipt tax, any discount, any service charge and the chosen additional tip into separate fields. Do not calculate a new tax rate when the receipt already gives the tax amount. Check whether a service charge or gratuity is already included before adding anything. A suggested tip printed on a receipt is a suggestion, not proof that it was paid.

Then agree which people share which items and how each extra component is divided. Equal tax is different from tax proportional to assigned items. An equal tip is different from a tip proportional to item shares. Neither choice follows automatically from dividing the meal. If you need to establish item ownership first, use the shared restaurant items guide.

A component ledger that finishes at exactly $38.50

In this fictional meal, A, B and C each own $10.00 of items. The receipt has $30.00 in items, no discount, $2.50 tax and no service charge. The group chooses a $6.00 tip, calculated here as 20% of the pre-tax $30.00 subtotal. That is this group’s declared choice, not a universal tipping basis. Total paid: $30.00 + $2.50 + $6.00 = $38.50.

Allocate items and tip evenly. Tax cannot be divided into three identical cent amounts. The group agrees that A takes the remaining tax cent today. This single declared residual rule produces the following ledger. The CSV contains these rows as editable values; it is not an automatic calculator.

The audit has two directions: each person’s components equal their owed amount, and each column equals its receipt component. Finally, $12.84 + $12.83 + $12.83 = $38.50. Record who received the extra cent and why. For later equal meals, the group can agree to rotate that person; do not silently change a past split.

When discounts, unequal items or payments change the answer

If a discount applies to one item only, identify its recipient before spreading anything across the group. If the group chooses a proportional allocation, write the denominator explicitly: for example, person’s assigned item subtotal divided by all assigned item subtotals. A different discount policy changes those numbers. Never label a pre-discount allocation as a post-discount one.

Check for unassigned items before trusting totals. An item that belongs to nobody in the allocation can disappear from the people’s sum while still existing on the receipt. Fix its assignment or its scanned price first; the wrong receipt price checklist handles that earlier step. A penny adjustment should not conceal a missing $8 dessert.

Keep owed and paid separate. If A pays the entire $38.50 by card, A does not owe $38.50 under this split. B and C each reimburse $12.83; A’s net cost is $12.84. Record transfers separately and check them against the final owed column. An unsettled transfer is not another restaurant expense.

Questions answered

Should I round each component or just the final share?

Choose one consistent rule before settling. This example allocates the actual tax into cent amounts once and checks both columns and final shares. Repeated independent rounding without reconciliation can create a gap.

Does BillLens automatically fix the last cent?

That guarantee was not established in the current source or listing. Compare the final people’s sum with the actual amount paid and document any agreed correction separately.

What if the difference is much larger than a cent?

Recheck missing items, quantities, discount basis, tax, service charge and tip. Do not use a residual-cent adjustment to hide a substantive mismatch.
