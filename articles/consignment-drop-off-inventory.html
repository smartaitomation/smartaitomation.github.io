Prepare a consignment drop-off list that reconciles custody and acceptance

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 27, 20267 min read

List each offered item before handover, then record what was accepted, held for inspection or returned and who physically has it. Reconcile all offered quantities. Drop-off, acceptance, sale and payment are separate events.

Prepare item identities before discussing prices

A bag count is not an item count. “One bag received” cannot tell you whether a coat, a lamp and a pair of shoes were all accepted. Give the owner one stable reference and each offered sale unit its own item ID. Include a short description, condition note and any matching photo reference. Keep personal contact details out of a worksheet you do not need to share.

Define the unit explicitly. A pair of shoes is one sale unit here, with two physical pieces. A set may need a component list. Do not compare “four items” from one person with “five pieces” from another and assume something vanished. Ask the shop what units it records and how you will match your IDs to its tags or receipt.

Read the actual shop’s current agreement before leaving property. Bushel & Peck’s published terms, for example, explicitly distinguish submission from acceptance. The Attic describes its own intake and receipt process. Those are examples of particular shops’ procedures, not rules imposed on every consignment business. This worksheet is a handover aid, not a legal agreement or proof that a shop must accept an item.

Four offered units, three different outcomes

In this original fictional handover, owner O-27 offers four sale units. Two are accepted, one remains with the shop awaiting inspection, and one is handed back immediately. “Pending” must describe an actual agreed hold, not a guess made after the owner leaves.

Check the offered-unit equation: 4 = 2 accepted + 1 pending + 1 returned. Check physical custody separately: the shop holds three units, while the owner has one back. The shop’s active accepted inventory is two units, not three or four. If the lamp is later accepted, change its decision with a dated reference; do not create another lamp.

Ask the receiving person to confirm the actual units and the receipt or message reference using the shop’s normal process. A note that you intend to ask is not an acknowledgement. If the shop only provides a bag receipt, preserve that limitation and seek item-level confirmation rather than marking every item accepted.

Resolve a pending item without losing the trail

For I02, record the agreed next step and actual contact date when one exists. Do not invent a universal inspection deadline. If the shop later declines the lamp, record whether it was physically collected, shipped back, or handled under another specifically agreed arrangement. “Declined” alone does not say where the lamp is.

If your offered count and the shop’s count differ, pause the reconciliation. Compare unit definitions, item IDs, tags, receipt and condition notes. A missing shoe within a pair is a component discrepancy even if both records say one sale unit. Keep an unresolved difference visible rather than changing your original offered list to make the totals match.

Do not infer a sale from acceptance or a payout from a listed price. Those later records need their own evidence. The inventory and payout guide covers the later workflow. If an accepted item comes back, the returned-item record guide handles that separate event.

Questions answered

Is a drop-off receipt proof that every item was accepted?

Not necessarily. Read what the actual receipt and shop agreement confirm. A bag or custody receipt may precede inspection and item-level acceptance.

Should pending items count as active inventory?

Keep pending custody distinct from accepted active inventory. Resolve the decision with a dated reference before moving the item into the accepted workflow.

Does this worksheet replace a consignment agreement?

No. It organizes item identity, observations and handover evidence. Use the actual shop agreement for its terms and obtain appropriate advice for legal questions.
