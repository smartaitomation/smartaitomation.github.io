Routines & accessibility · PictoCue

Simplify a visual schedule that has too many steps

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 24, 20267 min read

Find out which pictures and steps the person understands before reducing the display. Try fewer visible groups for an overview, but keep detailed steps available where they are still needed. Three cards are not inherently better than eight, and a shorter board is not useful if it hides the next action.

Ask what makes the current board difficult

Check whether the pictures are recognizable, the order makes sense, the board is accessible and the person can indicate what comes next. A long display may be the issue, but unclear symbols, an unrealistic routine or a missing choice can also explain difficulty. Do not assume that ignoring the board means unwillingness.

NHS guidance describes teaching the meaning and use of a visual timetable and notes that too much information at once may be overwhelming for some people. These are individualized support principles, not evidence that a fixed number of cards works for everyone or that PictoCue treats a condition.

Review the board with the person in a form they can use: pointing, selecting pictures, words or another familiar communication method. Ask which part feels unclear and what help they want. Keep necessary support rather than removing a step only to reach a smaller count.

Worked redesign: eight actions, three overview cards

Fictional original morning board has eight actions: choose clothes, put on top, put on bottoms, put on socks, put on shoes, pack bottle, pack bag and go to the door. The person already follows some dressing actions together but still needs individual packing cues.

The three overview cards cover all eight actions: four plus one plus three. This is a change in display level, not a claim that five actions disappeared. If “Get ready to leave” is too abstract, retain the three packing/door cards in view; do not count a shorter board as success while the task becomes less clear.

Try the revised display together and ask the person to indicate the next action. Record what needed clarification and which detail they asked to keep. A useful result might be two grouped sections plus visible packing steps, even though it does not equal three cards. The worked design is a candidate, not a universal prescription.

Review understanding rather than compliance counts

Choose one ordinary opportunity to see whether the person can use the revised cues with their preferred support. Note the observed difficulty without labeling it failure. Keep routine changes visible and respect requests for help, pause or a different format.

If one action becomes harder after grouping, restore its detail. A task can need more steps in one area and fewer in another. Keep pictures consistent when they already have an understood meaning, and teach any replacement instead of assuming the new artwork is self-explanatory.

Use the separate first/then guide when only two immediate activities need attention. This guide addresses display complexity across a broader routine; it does not set behavior targets, clinical outcomes or a mandatory independence standard.

Questions answered

Is three cards always better than eight?

No. Keep the amount of detail the person understands and wants.

Does grouping remove the underlying actions?

No. Keep a clear mapping and detailed cues where needed.

Does a generated board verify understanding?

No. Review the pictures and sequence together in an accessible way.
