Choose one visible question from a two-handed backhand clip

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 19, 20267 min read

Identify the stroke and handedness, then choose one checkpoint you can actually see. For the right-handed two-handed example here, note the visible top-arm finish and ask a coach how it relates to the intended action. If the view is blocked or cut short, record “not assessable” rather than turning uncertainty into a technique score.

Choose a backhand-specific question before watching

USTA’s two-handed backhand article discusses the non-dominant hand’s role and, for its right-handed example, a left-elbow finish across the net. That is the context for this review question, not a universal prescription for one-handed backhands or every player. Keep stroke type and handedness beside the note.

A camera can show a visible finish; it cannot directly measure how much force each hand contributed. Do not label a stroke “dominant-hand driven” merely because one elbow looks different. Ask a coach whether the observation is relevant to the intended stroke.

Review material you have permission to record and share. Keep other players and private conversations out of optional uploads where possible. A useful clip needs the relevant action visible without putting a camera or person in a dangerous place on court.

Worked review: two observations and one unassessable clip

Fictional comparison: the same right-handed player records three two-handed backhands from a consistent view. The one question is “Can I see where the left elbow finishes relative to the net after this hit?” These notes are hypothetical observations, not measurements from a real analyzed video.

Do not count A as a successful stroke, B as a failure and C as a zero score. The observation alone does not establish outcome, hand force or a technical cause. Record the incoming-ball and intended-shot differences before treating two clips as comparable.

If the elbow or net direction cannot be identified, say exactly what is missing. A new camera angle can change the apparent relationship. Preserve the view description so a later clip does not appear to demonstrate improvement merely because the camera moved.

Turn the note into one coach question

Bring the clip, the checkpoint and the uncertainty together: “In this right-handed two-handed backhand, the finish is visible here. Does this checkpoint help explain the action you want me to practice?” That gives a coach something narrower to answer than “fix my backhand.”

Ask which one cue to retain in practice and what would show that you understood it. Do not add grip changes, body angles, faster swings or a new drill simply to make the worksheet look complete. This page does not prescribe a training dose or assess injury risk.

Keep the original and your later notes separately. If feedback changes your interpretation, add a dated revision instead of rewriting the original observation as though you knew the answer then. A short clip remains partial evidence of a larger movement and playing situation.

Questions answered

Can video show which hand supplied the force?

Not directly from this visual checkpoint. Keep the visible observation separate from a force claim.

What if I play a one-handed backhand?

This USTA-based right-handed two-handed example does not prescribe your technique; ask for a suitable checkpoint.

Is an occluded finish a failed stroke?

No. It is not assessable from that clip.
