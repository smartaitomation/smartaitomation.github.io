Make a first–then board for one clearly bounded transition

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 18, 20267 min read

Show one short, concrete first action and what will actually happen next, using pictures the person recognizes. Define how the first action ends, explain the board together and change it openly if the next activity becomes unavailable. A board communicates a plan; it does not guarantee compliance or treat distress.

Choose a smaller task than “get ready”

Begin with the moment that becomes confusing. A whole morning schedule can hide the actual transition: leaving a construction game to get shoes. Write what the person needs to understand, not a judgment about their behavior. Choose one observable action with a clear finish.

The Norfolk NHS guidance describes two visually represented activities: first and then. Objects, photos, pictures or symbols can communicate the sequence. That supports a communication format, not a claim that our app was clinically tested or that every child will respond in the same way.

Choose recognizable images with the person and keep the spoken words consistent with the pictures. A detailed decorative drawing may be less useful than a familiar photo. Ask or observe whether the picture means what you intend; do not assume recognition because an adult likes the artwork.

Worked transition: two blocks, then a real next activity

Fictional situation: a child is building on the floor before going outside. “Tidy everything” is too vague for this first attempt. The proposed first action is “Put these two blocks in the box.” The next activity is “Choose your shoes.” The adult has the box and shoes ready. This is a small communication example, not a behavior protocol.

Show the board before the transition and pair it with brief, consistent words. Acknowledge the first action when it is finished. Do not keep adding extra actions under the same first card; that changes the promise the picture communicated.

If the next activity cannot happen, say so and update the board with the person. Keep a backup wording such as “The shoes are not here. Let’s choose what happens next.” Do not present an unavailable activity as an inducement.

Record understanding, not a success score

Afterward, note what was understood, what needed explanation and what was missing. “Pointed at shoes; needed help finding the box” is more useful than “failed.” Separate a picture mismatch from an unavailable object or an action that was too large.

Respond to distress and communication needs rather than escalating the board into a demand. Do not withhold basic needs or support behind completion. If transitions remain difficult, discuss the person’s needs with appropriate caregivers or professionals instead of assuming more pictures will solve the cause.

The CSV helps compare one small revision at a time. Keep the same transition if you are checking a new picture, and note other changes. It is an observation aid, not a diagnostic instrument or evidence of a treatment effect.

Questions answered

Does the board guarantee cooperation?

No. It communicates a sequence and cannot establish why someone is distressed.

What if the next activity is unavailable?

Explain and change the plan openly; do not keep displaying a promise you cannot fulfill.

Can I use a paper version?

Yes. Use recognizable pictures or objects and the same clear words without an app.
