Place a phone for a useful sleep-sound recording

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 12, 20266 min read

Choose a stable, uncovered surface where the phone and any charger remain ventilated. Check that bedding cannot cover the device, then make a short awake test from your usual bed position. Record the placement and background sounds so a later noisy clip has context. There is no universal distance here and no recording position that turns an app into a medical sleep test.

Start with safety, not a claimed perfect distance

Follow the safety instructions for your actual iPhone, charger and room. Apple says to keep the phone, adapter and wireless charger ventilated during use or charging, and not to sleep on or cover powered equipment with a pillow, blanket or your body. An uncovered bedside surface can be a candidate; it is not automatically suitable if bedding, a spill, a pet or a dangling cable can reach it.

Inspect the whole arrangement while awake. Keep the device secure on its surface and check where its cable and adapter sit. Do not tuck a microphone under a pillow to make a voice louder. Do not move prescribed medical equipment or disconnect it to make the recording quieter. If you use an implanted medical device, follow your clinician and device manufacturer’s guidance about nearby electronics.

A simple floor-plan note is enough: “right bedside surface; phone uncovered; fan near door.” No centimetre measurement in this guide has been validated for Noctory. A short listening test checks audible conditions, not electrical safety or sleep-monitor accuracy.

Use an awake test to isolate one change

Use a recorder you know how to stop and replay. Check its permissions and any free-use allowance before a test; a short session may count against an app’s recording allowance. With everyone’s agreement, speak the same ordinary sentence from the same position while awake, stop, and listen. Do not record another person secretly.

Compare two safe arrangements while changing only one factor: placement, for example. Keep the speaker position and normal room equipment unchanged. Save plain observations such as “words audible; fan steady” or “movement knocks dominate.” Avoid treating playback loudness as a measured decibel level. A clearer voice test does not prove snoring detection or identify the source of every overnight sound.

Keep nighttime context with the clips

Before bed, note the chosen surface, whether the normal fan or sound machine is operating, who has agreed to recording, and any unusual background event. In the morning, add the recording start/stop times and interruptions you actually know. If the phone moved, mark that night as a changed setup.

Listen to a clip before naming it. A nearby person, a pet, traffic or an appliance can contribute sound. “Possible source” and “confirmed source while awake” are different labels. A sound-classification label cannot establish who made a noise or whether a person was asleep.

Use similar setups when comparing nights, and retain exceptions. More labeled clips could reflect a different room, sensitivity or recording duration; it is not proof that health worsened. If you have breathing or sleep concerns, discuss them with a healthcare professional instead of using this placement sheet to diagnose or exclude a condition.

Questions answered

Should I put the phone under a pillow?

Do not cover powered equipment with bedding. Follow Apple’s ventilation and heat-exposure guidance for the phone and charger.

What distance gives accurate snoring results?

This guide has no validated distance or clinical accuracy claim. Use a safe, repeatable setup and listen to an awake test.

Does no recorded snoring mean no sleep problem?

No. Recording and sound labels cannot diagnose or rule out a sleep disorder.
