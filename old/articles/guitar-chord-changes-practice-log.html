Practice one difficult chord change and record what improves

By Smart Automation SRLOctober 11, 20266 min read

Pick one chord pair, define the fingering and strum pattern, then count changes that arrive on the intended beat with the expected strings ringing clearly. Keep those conditions fixed while testing a tempo change. A larger attempt count is not progress if the chords become late or muted.

Choose a pair you can actually inspect

Write both chord shapes and the fingering you intend to use. “G to D” is more precise than “practice chords,” but even that pair has alternate fingerings. Use the same version while making the comparison. Check each chord separately before asking the hand to move between them.

Fender’s chord-change guidance recommends slow metronome practice and noticing finger movement, including shared fingers where the selected shapes permit. That supports a focused transition exercise; it does not validate the tempo, repetition count or results in this original worksheet.

Choose a simple pattern that lets you hear the landing. For the example, play one chord for four quarter-note beats, then change to the other on the next beat one. Count one move G→D or D→G as one attempt; a round trip is two. Keep the same strumming direction and intended strings. Do not add a new rhythm, capo or alternate fingering while testing the tempo.

Worked example: attempts and clean changes are different

Use a target such as “lands on beat one, without an unintended muted string.” You judge the result by listening. Stop if you experience pain and seek appropriate technique or clinical guidance for a recurring concern. The following values are invented, not recommended speed targets.

The second block gives a clearer place to work, but it is not proof of lasting skill improvement. A tempo change itself can explain the difference. Keep results at different tempos separate; compare repeated blocks at the same tempo when asking whether that exact task is becoming more reliable.

Use the failure location to choose the next action

A helpful note names what you heard or felt: “D arrived after beat one,” “one intended string was muted,” or “I stopped strumming while moving.” Do not change all three at once. Pick one cue for the next block and keep the other conditions stable.

If each chord is unclear even without a change, work on the individual shape before treating the problem as transition speed. If the isolated pair is reliable but a song breaks down, note what changed: preceding chord, rhythm, tempo or phrase length. The song is a different test, so do not overwrite the pair’s result.

End with an actionable line, such as “repeat the same G/D shapes at 52 with an on-time D landing.” Avoid “get better tomorrow” or promising a deadline. A teacher can help identify unnecessary movement or an unsuitable fingering; the worksheet only preserves observations for that conversation.

Questions answered

Should I count every attempt as a clean change?

No. Keep attempts and changes meeting your defined cue separate so faster but unclear playing does not look like progress.

Can I compare results at different tempos directly?

Record both, but the tempo change affects difficulty. Repeat the same conditions for a more useful personal comparison.

Does RiffSprint listen and grade the chords?

Its current listing says it does not record microphone audio. Do not expect automatic verification of the clean-change counts.
